Ternary phase diagrams for polymer / solvent / non-solvent systems

This page computes Flory-Huggins phase diagrams: the binodal, the spinodal and the tie-lines. Choose a system, change the parameters if you like, and press Compute. Click on the diagram to test a composition. For the two Torlon systems with 2-propanol the parameters are fitted to published cloud points (Ishaque 2020). For the other systems they are rough estimates.

About the data. The only measured data on this page are nine published cloud points for Torlon 4000HV with 2-propanol (Ishaque 2020), shown as squares for the two Torlon systems. Everything else comes from theory and handbook values: the Flory-Huggins model, interaction parameters estimated from published Hansen solubility parameters, and rough estimates for the other systems.

How to read the diagram

Each point in the triangle is one composition, and the three percentages add up to 100. Read each component along its own axis. Its grid lines run parallel to the side opposite its corner.

  • Outside the binodal (red line): one phase. The solution is clear and stable.
  • Between the binodal and the spinodal (blue dashed line): metastable. The solution separates by nucleation and growth of the second phase, which can take time.
  • Inside the spinodal: unstable. The solution separates spontaneously, typically into an interconnected structure (spinodal decomposition).

Inside the grey region a composition splits into two phases. A tie-line joins the two phases that coexist, and the position of a composition along its tie-line gives the share of each phase (the lever rule).

In immersion precipitation, solvent leaves the cast film while non-solvent enters, so the composition moves from the solvent and polymer side toward the polymer and non-solvent side. If it enters the two-phase region at a higher polymer content than the critical point, the polymer-lean phase nucleates, which usually gives a porous, sponge-like structure. Below the critical point the polymer-rich phase nucleates as droplets, which tends to give a weak, nodular structure. In a cloud-point titration, non-solvent is added to a polymer solution, so the composition moves straight toward the non-solvent corner until the solution turns turbid.

Casting: dope, bath and mixing line

The Casting box takes a dope (the casting solution) and a coagulation bath of solvent and non-solvent. It draws the dope as a diamond and the straight line from the dope to the bath. Every mixture of the two lies on that line, by mass balance. The line is straight in volume fractions, because the lattice model has no volume change on mixing, and it is also straight in weight fractions. The box reports three numbers:

  • Cloud point of the dope. How much more non-solvent the dope can take before it turns turbid. A small margin means the dope is close to the binodal. Adding non-solvent to the dope in this way is known to shift demixing toward instantaneous and to thin the skin (Reuvers and Smolders 1987, Part II).
  • Entry point. Where the mixing line enters the two-phase region, and at what polymer content compared with the critical point.
  • Bath limit. The most solvent the bath can hold while the mixing line still reaches the two-phase region. Solvent in the bath slows down precipitation (Wijmans, Baaij and Smolders 1983). Past this limit the overall mixture stays one phase in the model.

The mixing line is not the path that the film follows. During immersion, solvent diffuses out faster or slower than non-solvent diffuses in, so the polymer content near the surface rises first and the local composition follows a curved path that depends on time and on depth in the film (Reuvers, van den Berg and Smolders 1987, Part I). The mixing line only shows where the whole system ends up. The bath limit is therefore a guide to the order of magnitude, not a sharp threshold. Whether demixing is instantaneous or delayed, and the structure it gives, needs the diffusion model, and this page does not solve it.

Fast or slow demixing

When a cast film is immersed, liquid-liquid demixing either starts at once (instantaneous demixing) or only after a delay. The two give different membranes. For instantaneously precipitating films "a thin toplayer of a nodular structure is always present", and these are also the cases where macrovoids form. With delayed demixing "a membrane with a dense toplayer is the result", and membranes without macrovoids form, "except when the delay time is very short" (Smolders et al. 1992). Reviews describe the first as a finger-like substructure with a thin skin and the second as a sponge-like substructure with a relatively dense top layer (Tan and Rodrigue 2019).

The Casting box lists the factors that push the chosen dope and bath one way or the other, each with its source:

  • Solvent in the bath slows precipitation (Wijmans, Baaij and Smolders 1983). With dioxane, DMF or DMSO "one has to add increasing amounts of these solvents to the coagulation bath, in order to enforce delayed demixing" (Smolders et al. 1992).
  • Non-solvent in the dope makes demixing start sooner, down to instantaneous demixing with a very thin skin (Reuvers and Smolders 1987, Part II). It also suppresses the local delay that lets macrovoids grow (Smolders et al. 1992).
  • Mutual affinity of solvent and non-solvent. High affinity is likely to give a more porous membrane, and low affinity is more likely to delay demixing and give a dense top layer (Tan and Rodrigue 2019). Going from THF to DMSO the miscibility with water increases (Smolders et al. 1992). The page ranks the solvents by χ12 with the chosen non-solvent. Except for 2-propanol, these χ12 values are rough estimates.
  • The non-solvent. For polysulfone in DMAc, changing the non-solvent from water to 2-propanol or ethanol changed demixing from instantaneous to delayed, and the macrovoids disappeared (Smolders et al. 1992).
  • Polymer content. More polymer suppresses macrovoids (Smolders et al. 1992; Guillen et al. 2013) and thickens the top layer (Mulder et al. 1985). One review argues the opposite for the onset of demixing, that more polymer makes instantaneous demixing more likely (Tan and Rodrigue 2019). The page therefore shows polymer content as a note, without a direction.

When all directional factors agree, the box names a tendency. When they disagree, it says so. The indicator does not compute a delay time. That needs the diffusion model of Reuvers et al. (1987), which this page does not solve.

Recipe suggestions

The Recipe box takes a target structure and returns a range for each setting. Two numbers come from the diagram of the current system. The lower limit on polymer is the smallest content for which the mixing line toward a pure non-solvent bath enters the two-phase region above the critical point, so that the polymer-lean phase nucleates and a porous structure can form. The upper limits on bath solvent and on non-solvent in the dope are the bath limit and the cloud point from the Casting box. Everything else is a direction taken from the papers above. The page does not invent target values inside these ranges; the right value depends on the film thickness, the temperature and the kinetics, and has to be found by experiment.

Porosity

No published formula was found that predicts the porosity of a membrane from the dope and bath compositions for the systems on this page, so the page gives no predicted porosity. What it does give is the standard way to measure porosity from two weighings (Ali et al. 2018, Eq. 1): 

ε = [(w1 − w2) / ρL] / { [(w1 − w2) / ρL] + w2 / ρP }

with w1 the mass of the membrane wet with a liquid of density ρL (Ali et al. used kerosene, 0.82 g/cm³), w2 the dry mass and ρP the polymer density (1.78 g/cm³ for PVDF in that paper). The Porosity box evaluates it; the polymer density is filled in from the chosen system.

Pore-forming additives

PVP and PEG are added to the dope to change the structure. The model on this page has three components, so it does not include them. The published trends are:

  • PVP and macrovoids. Adding PVP to PES / NMP / water "suppresses the formation of macrovoids in the sub-layer", but only above "a certain minimum concentration of polymeric additive" and a minimum molar mass of both polymers. The result is better pore interconnectivity and higher porosity (Boom et al. 1992). For PES with up to 3 wt % PVP the structure stayed channel-like, and 5 to 8 wt % PVP moved it toward sponge-like (Salehi Shahrabi et al. 2017). Not every study agrees: one PES study found that added PVP enhanced macrovoids and increased porosity (Nisa et al. 2026).
  • Viscosity. Both additives raise the viscosity of the dope, which hinders demixing kinetically (Aminudin et al. 2013). In one PES dope, 3 wt % PVP K90 raised the viscosity from 276 to 1099 cP, and 10 wt % PEG 400 to 429 cP (Salehi Shahrabi et al. 2017).
  • Porosity and flux. PEG and PVP both increased the water permeation of polysulfone membranes (Aminudin et al. 2013). For PEI, porosity increased with PEG content (Chen et al. 2010). For PES, porosity went through a maximum near 3 wt % PVP (Salehi Shahrabi et al. 2017).
  • Molar mass of PEG. Flux and water permeability were highest with medium-size PEG and fell again with very high molar mass PEG (Méndez et al. 2014).

Why PEI is not in the calculator yet. The obvious way to estimate χ is from Hansen solubility parameters, χ = V/(RT) [(ΔδD)² + ¼ (ΔδP)² + ¼ (ΔδH)²] (Hansen 2007). This is how χ12 for 2-propanol is estimated on this page. For Ultem the published values are δD = 21.1, δP = 6.7 and δH = 8.1 MPa1/2 (Syeda et al. 2024, from Hansen 2007 and Chung and Xu 1998). With NMP (18.0, 12.3 and 7.2 MPa1/2, molar volume 96.5 cm³/mol) at 25 °C this gives χ23 = 0.69. That is above the value at which a long-chain polymer stops dissolving (0.54 for n3 = 600), so the model would say NMP does not dissolve Ultem. In practice Ultem is cast from NMP. The Hansen estimate is therefore not good enough for χ23 here, and PEI will be added once published interaction parameters or cloud points are found.

PBI and PVC. For these the open literature gave too little to set the three χ values. For PVC in DMAc with water at 30 °C, one cloud point without additives is published as a table (10.6 wt % PVC, 85.6 wt % DMAc, 3.8 wt % water; Padilha and Borges 2019). One point cannot fix three parameters. For PBI only Hansen parameters were found, which have the same problem as for PEI. Both will be added if published interaction parameters or cloud-point series are found.

The other polymers in the list that the calculator covers use rough estimates, except Torlon. Their diagrams show the shape and the trends, not exact numbers.

Fit to measured cloud points

Cloud points of Torlon 4000HV in NMP and in DMF, titrated with 2-propanol, are given in Ishaque (2020), Figure 36. The points were read off that figure, taken as weight percent, and converted to volume fractions. The model was then fitted to these points. This is a calibration, not an independent test: the model is compared with the same nine points it was fitted to.

The fit is not unique. Any χ13 from about 1.4 to 1.8 fits within 1 wt % if χ23 is adjusted with it. Over that range χ23 for NMP goes from about 0.46 to below zero, while χ23 for DMF only goes from about 0.52 to 0.44. Two results hold over the whole range. χ13 must be at least about 1.3 to 1.4. And DMF is always the weaker solvent for Torlon, not far from the value at which it would stop dissolving long chains (χ23 = 0.54 for n3 = 600). In the fit χ23 was kept between −0.3 and that limit. These conclusions depend on the Hansen estimates for χ12. An independent measurement, for example the swelling of Torlon in 2-propanol, would be needed to fix the individual values.

The points were read from a figure, so they carry about ±0.5 wt % of reading error. The figure does not state its units. If the source tables are in volume percent, the fit has to be redone.

Method

Flory-Huggins free energy of mixing per lattice site, for non-solvent (1), solvent (2) and polymer (3) with volume fractions φi: 

ΔG_m / RT = φ1 ln φ1 + φ2 ln φ2 + (φ3/n3) ln φ3
          + χ12 φ1 φ2 + χ13 φ1 φ3 + χ23 φ2 φ3

Binodal. Two phases coexist when all three chemical potentials are equal in both. The program starts on the polymer / non-solvent side, where the problem is a binary one, and then follows the binodal into the triangle one tie-line at a time. At each step it fixes the polymer content of the polymer-rich phase and solves the three equations with Newton's method. It works with the logarithms of small fractions, because the polymer-lean phase can contain less than 10−300 polymer. The steps get smaller as the tie-lines get shorter, and the calculation stops where the tie-line shrinks to a point, at the critical point.

Spinodal. The limit of local stability, where the determinant of the second derivatives of ΔGm is zero: 

G22 G33 − G23² = 0
G22 = 1/φ1 + 1/φ2 − 2 χ12
G33 = 1/φ1 + 1/(n3 φ3) − 2 χ13
G23 = 1/φ1 − χ12 − χ13 + χ23

Checks. On every computed tie-line the chemical potentials agree to within about 10−10 per lattice site, and the binodal closes where the spinodal determinant passes through zero, as it must at a critical point. The solver was compared with an independent implementation in Python (SciPy): tie-lines agree to better than 10−7, critical points to about 3 × 10−5, and the fitted cloud points to 0.1 wt %. When you click a point in the two-phase region, the tie-line through it is solved exactly, not interpolated.

Downloads. The three buttons under Download save the current diagram as CSV files, with every composition in wt % and in vol %. The header lines give the system, the parameters and the densities used to convert volume fractions to weight fractions. The binodal file has the polymer-rich branch, the critical point and the polymer-lean branch in order, so it plots as one closed curve. The tie-line file has every computed tie-line, not only the ones drawn. The spinodal file is computed over the whole triangle, whatever part is shown. Each spinodal point is found by bisection on the determinant above, so it lies on the spinodal to rounding error. The checks read the files back: the chemical potentials in the two phases of each tie-line still agree to better than 10−9, and the spinodal passes through the critical point.

Limitations

The χ parameters are constant, with no dependence on composition or temperature. Both small molecules occupy one lattice site, the polymer has a single chain length, and there is no ternary interaction term. These are the textbook assumptions. They work poorly for water-based systems, where the interaction parameters depend strongly on composition. Altena and Smolders, and Romay, Diban and Urtiaga, use a composition-dependent non-solvent / solvent parameter for this reason, and Yilmaz and McHugh show how sensitive the diagram is to the interaction parameters and their concentration dependence. For the water systems in the list, χ13 is set between 2 and 3, which puts the cloud points at a few to about 20 wt % water, the usual order of magnitude. These values are not fitted to data.

The program traces the two-phase region that starts on the polymer / non-solvent side, which is the normal case for membrane casting. It gives a note when the solvent and non-solvent would separate on their own, when the solvent is too poor to dissolve the polymer, or when no two-phase region starts on that side.
